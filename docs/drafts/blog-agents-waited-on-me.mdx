**TL;DR**

- In one week, 65 Claude Code and Codex sessions on my laptop ended a turn and waited for me 285 times — 2,372 minutes of summed waiting.
- I expected the long waits to come from running too many agents at once. Bucketing every wait by how many sessions were in play said the opposite: 25% of waits ran past 10 minutes with 1–2 sessions in play, versus 11% with 3 or more.
- The real lesson is that "agents wait on the human" is two different problems with opposite fixes, and you cannot tell which one you have without counting.

You start four agents, go make coffee, come back, and three of them have been sitting on a finished turn for twenty minutes. The obvious conclusion is that you are running more agents than you can review. Cap the parallelism, problem solved.

That was my plan. I had the config key half-written. Then I measured it first.

## What the radar actually counts

The [office radar](https://github.com/KelvinYou/personal-os) in Personal-OS reads the transcripts that Codex and Claude Code already write to disk — read-only, no prompt text, no tool arguments, just timestamps and state labels. Every session gets one of four attention zones: *needs you*, *working*, *review*, *done*.

A **wait** opens when a session needs me — a turn ended, or it asked for input or permission — and closes at my next prompt in that session. Waits over four hours count as abandoned and drop out of the totals.

The part that answers the question is one function. When a wait opens, it counts how many sessions were holding my attention at that instant:

```python
def in_play(timelines, at, cfg) -> int:
    count = 0
    for points in timelines.values():
        covering = _phase_at(points, at)
        if covering is None: continue
        start, phase, following = covering
        age = (at - start).total_seconds()
        if phase in RUNNING and (following is not None or age <= cfg.stale_after_seconds): count += 1
        elif phase == "input" and age <= cfg.wait_cap_seconds: count += 1
        elif phase == "ended" and age <= cfg.review_window_seconds: count += 1
    return count
```

Running agents count. Unanswered input requests count. A turn that ended recently and is sitting in review counts. The session that opened the wait counts itself, so the minimum is 1.

Then the weekly report compares the share of long waits (over 10 minutes) at 1–2 in play against 3 or more, and prints whichever reading the data supports. Both sentences are in the code; neither is the default.

The median wait was 1.1 minutes. Most of the time I answer almost immediately. The damage is all in the tail — p90 of 19.2 minutes, a longest single wait of 186 minutes — and the tail lives at the *low* end of parallelism.

## Why the obvious diagnosis was wrong

When I have four or five sessions going, I am at my desk, switching between them. A finished turn gets seen within a minute because I am already looking.

When I have one or two going, it is usually because I kicked off something long and left — lunch, a meeting, the commute. The session finishes while I am away, and it waits until I happen to come back.

A session cap attacks the first situation, which was not the problem. It does nothing for the second, because there is nothing to cap. What shortens a wait while I am away from the desk is a signal that reaches me away from the desk. So the change that shipped was a Claude Code `Notification` hook that pushes to my phone through ntfy: a permission prompt left untouched for 90 seconds, or a turn that ended and got no input for about a minute. The push text is fixed — no session name, path or command leaves the laptop. Codex sessions are not covered yet.

Two honesty notes on the numbers. The 2,372 minutes is summed across parallel sessions, so it measures agent-time spent waiting; the hours it cost my own day are far fewer. And 31 of the 65 sessions had only partial history in the scan window, so the per-bucket counts are a floor.

## The counter-argument I take seriously

*A wait nobody pays for is not a cost.* If I deliberately left a long task running over lunch, the agent sitting idle for forty minutes cost me nothing — I was not going to review it anyway. Counting it as "waiting on the human" inflates a number that does not matter.

That is partly right, and it is why the median is the headline and the total is not. But it misses where the time goes. A finished turn usually ends with a question or a permission prompt, and the next turn cannot start until I answer it. A forty-minute wait on a session that needed a ten-second "yes" is forty minutes the rest of the task did not run. The phone alert lets those cheap answers happen without a desk.

What I cannot show you yet is the after-picture. The push hook shipped on 8 November. One day of data is not evidence, and I will publish the next full week whether or not the tail moves.

## The transferable part

**Split the waiting problem before you fix it.** "Too many agents to review" and "agent finished while I was away" produce the same symptom and need opposite fixes — a cap for one, a reachable alert for the other. Bucketing waits by sessions in play separates them in one table.

**Let the report pick the reading.** The weekly line is computed from the comparison, with both conclusions written in code. If next month the long waits move to 3+ in play, the same report will tell me to cap sessions, and I will not have to remember to re-check my own assumption.

**Count from records the tools already write.** Nothing here needed an agent framework or a wrapper around the CLI. Codex and Claude Code both leave transcripts on disk; one hook adds exact permission-prompt times. Observation that needs no client changes is observation you will still be running in three months.
